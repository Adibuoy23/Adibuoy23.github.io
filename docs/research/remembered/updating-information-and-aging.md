# How do we update information when comprehending narratives, and how does that change as we age?

Reading a story means keeping a running model of it in your head — who is there, where
they are, what is going on. When the story shifts, that model has to change with it. The
interesting question is what happens to everything you were already holding.

There are two ways it could work. You might update **incrementally**, swapping out only the
part that changed and keeping the rest intact. Or you might update **globally**, clearing
the whole model and rebuilding it, whether or not any given detail actually changed.

A simple trick tells them apart. Read a sentence — *"Jim had been standing for five minutes
and already his achy back was bothering him"* — then a few filler sentences, then a shift:
*"Jim took his receipt and they drove to the grocery store."* Now, was "achy back" in the
story? If only the changed part was cleared, you should be slow only on probes about what
changed. If everything was cleared, you should be slow on all of it.

Twenty younger and twenty older adults read six stories this way — a hospital visit, a
castle tour, a camping trip, a morning at home, a trip to the zoo, a day at the office —
while we scanned them.

**The two groups updated differently.** Younger adults slowed selectively, for probes on the
dimension that had just shifted: a mix of incremental and global updating. Older adults
slowed for everything, changed or unchanged alike — the signature of global updating, as
though the whole model were rebuilt each time. Accuracy was the same in both groups, so this
is about how quickly information can be reached, not whether it survives at all.

**The brain data only half agree.** Across the default mode network — precuneus, medial
prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, angular gyrus — older adults showed elevated, more sustained
responses than younger adults, both at the shifts themselves and at the memory probes. But
while younger adults' probe-locked activity tracked their behavioural profile, older adults'
activity did not favour either account. Their behaviour said "global"; their brains did not
say much either way.

One more wrinkle worth flagging: nearly all of this was carried by **character** probes.
Neither group showed the effect for spatial ones. That may be because the protagonist is the
axis a story is organised around — or simply because place is usually mentioned once, in
passing ("by the mirror"), while characters are named again and again, so spatial detail is
the first thing to slip.

*Upadhyayula, Bailey, Su, Flores, Reagh & Zacks (in preparation).*
